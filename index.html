Who I Am

I'm an early-career software developer and BSc GeoInformatics: Computer Science graduate from Stellenbosch University, currently completing an Honours degree. My work sits at the intersection of software development and spatial data science, building mobile applications for field teams, developing intelligent systems and applying machine learning to geospatial health datasets.

My Honours thesis applies Python-based machine learning and geospatial analysis to predict tuberculosis notification risk at clinic and ward level across South Africa - research that combines programming rigour with GIS principles in a real-world public health context.

I have hands-on experience in remote sensing and Earth Observation, including satellite-derived vegetation indices (NDVI and related EO metrics), working with ArcGIS, QGIS, eCognition Developer, and Google Earth Engine for spatial analysis. A co-authored paper using EO to assess the effects of warfare on vegetation in Gaza was presented at the International Conference of Military GeoScience 2024.

Alongside my studies, I serve as a Lecture Assistant at Stellenbosch University across multiple GIT modules, supporting first- to third-year students. I also tutor computer science students through Magane CS Tutors and contribute back-end development work at POS IT Excellence.

// 04 — reflection

Academic Development

A portfolio is not just a record of completed work — it is evidence of growth. The following reflects honestly on where I began, what I have developed, and where I am still growing.

Before this work, I could…

Where I Started

When I began my studies, I could write basic Java and Python programs and had a surface-level understanding of GIS as a mapping tool. My projects were small and self-contained. This became tangible in my first GIT practicals, where I could produce a map but had no framework for asking what the map was actually for, or whether the data behind it was appropriate. I had not yet built software that integrated multiple technologies, handled real user constraints, or required thinking about field conditions, network connectivity, or spatial data at scale — the gap between academic exercises and real problems was something I could not yet see.

Now I can…

Where I Am Now

I can build full-stack and mobile applications, design authenticated systems for low-connectivity environments, implement intelligent game-playing algorithms, and apply machine learning to geospatial datasets. More significantly, I have come to understand that technical skill is only meaningful in context — the most important shift has been learning to ask who uses the system, under what conditions, and what the data actually represents. That reorientation, from building things that work to building things that work for someone, defines how I approach problems now. I can also communicate technical GIS work to a professional academic audience, which has sharpened my ability to justify methodological choices rather than simply execute them.

This is shown in…

The Evidence

The Gendac routing application required me to design for field conditions I had not previously encountered — secure authentication, session management, and offline caching for users with intermittent connectivity. What it revealed was that the hardest design decisions were not technical but contextual: understanding the user's environment was the actual problem. My Honours thesis extends this further: applying spatial ML to predict TB notification risk taught me that model output is only as defensible as the spatial and epidemiological reasoning behind it — programming skill alone is insufficient. Presenting at ICMG 2024 was the clearest evidence of professional growth: communicating original geospatial findings to an expert international audience forced precision I had not previously needed, and demonstrated that I could situate my own work within a broader disciplinary conversation.

I still need to improve…

What's Next

My geospatial analysis pipeline is still developing, and two areas are clear priorities. First, I need to work more rigorously with uncertainty quantification in spatial models — specifically, moving beyond reporting accuracy metrics to critically evaluating where and why a model breaks down spatially. I will address this directly through my thesis, by mapping residuals geographically and interrogating their spatial structure rather than treating them as aggregate statistics. Second, I want to build fluency in production-level data engineering for large spatial datasets — working through real pipeline failures, not just controlled academic examples. I will know I have made meaningful progress when I can defend a model's limitations to a domain expert, not just describe its performance.
